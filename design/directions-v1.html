Three directions for the overlay

Handrail lives on top of somebody else's software. That single fact drives every decision below — it has to be readable over a black timeline and a white spreadsheet, it must never be mistaken for part of the host app, and it has to reassure someone who is already unsure of what they're doing. Pick one.

The real decision The signature colour. The arrow is the product, so whatever colour it is becomes the brand.
Ruled out up front Blue — every host app uses it for selection and focus. Red and amber — they read as errors.
Tested against A dark editor (Premiere, Unreal, After Effects) and a light spreadsheet (Excel).
Audience Non-technical. Someone who can follow instructions but can't diagnose why step 4 failed.
Direction A

Calm Instrument

A precision tool that stays out of the way. Dark translucent glass, tight type, one cool accent used sparingly. It borrows the restraint of the professional software it sits on, so it reads as equipment rather than as an app that wandered onto the screen.

Direction C

The Rail

Takes the name literally. Instead of floating in the middle of the screen, Handrail docks to an edge — a rail you follow along. Steps become a visible spine you can see progress down, and the centre of the screen, where the work is, stays clear.

What picking one actually decides

The arrow's colour

It's the thing people will see in a screenshot of Handrail, so it's the brand whether or not we treat it as one.

Where the overlay lives

A and B float and can be dragged anywhere. C docks to an edge and stays there. This is not a detail — it changes the IPC contract and the window model.

Who the product sounds like

A is equipment. B is a person helping. C is a route you're following. The copy changes with the choice.

What onboarding looks like

The API-key screen you asked for gets designed in whichever language wins, along with the three states.

My read. B serves the audience thesis best and A is the safest, but C is the only one that would make somebody stop scrolling — it comes from the product's own name, it solves the occlusion problem the other two have, and no competitor does it.

The catch. C contradicts the three-state model already locked in PRODUCT.md, so choosing it means reversing that decision deliberately rather than by accident.

A hybrid is available. C's rail for guided multi-step tasks, A or B's floating bar for quick questions — the product already has two modes, and they could have two shapes. More to build, but it's the honest answer to a hybrid task model.
